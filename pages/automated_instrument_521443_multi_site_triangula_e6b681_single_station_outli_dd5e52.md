## Introduction

Automated instrumented UFO or UAP detectors are designed to find unusual observations, not to assume that every unusual observation represents an unusual object. A track that looks exceptional in a single camera can become entirely ordinary once its distance is measured. This is because a lone optical station records only where an object appears on the image plane, not how far away it is. Without range, estimates of speed, altitude, size and [acceleration]({{ 'acceleration/' | relative_url }}) are largely inferred rather than measured. Multi-site triangulation exists precisely to resolve this ambiguity by converting two-dimensional image tracks into three-dimensional positions with quantified [uncertainty]({{ 'uncertainty/' | relative_url }}). NASA's independent UAP study highlighted the need for calibrated, multi-sensor observations because isolated sightings often lack the information needed to place reliable physical constraints on an event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Scientific Investigation of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) Using Multimodal Ground-Based ObservatoriesMay 29, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2023</span></span></span>

## What image-plane motion can and cannot prove

A camera converts the three-dimensional sky into a flat image. Every detection is reduced to a changing pixel position or an angular direction. Even with excellent tracking software, a single station cannot determine where along that viewing direction the object actually is.

This limitation creates an important distinction between an observational outlier and a physical outlier. A detection may look unusual because:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* it crosses the field of view rapidly;
* it appears to reverse direction;
* it follows an unexpected curve;
* it brightens or dims abruptly.

</div>

None of these observations alone determines whether the object itself performed an extraordinary manoeuvre. The same image-plane behaviour can arise from widely different physical situations, including a nearby insect passing close to the lens, a bird changing direction, a drone at moderate range, an aircraft at cruising altitude, or a satellite hundreds of kilometres away. Without distance, the camera records only angular motion rather than true motion through space.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv+2Geodetic Systems]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Scientific Investigation of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) Using Multimodal Ground-Based ObservatoriesMay 29, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2023</span></span></span>

For automated detection systems this distinction is crucial. Algorithms are intentionally tuned to identify statistical outliers so that nothing potentially interesting is missed. Being flagged as an outlier therefore means "different from the background population", not "physically anomalous".

## Why distance changes apparent speed

The same angular movement corresponds to vastly different physical velocities depending on range.

Imagine two objects each moving across one degree of sky in one second. If one object is only 100 metres away while the other is 10 kilometres distant, their true sideways speeds differ by roughly two orders of magnitude despite producing nearly identical camera tracks.

The opposite effect also occurs. A nearby object can sweep rapidly across a camera's field of view while moving quite slowly in reality. Small insects close to wide-angle lenses are a familiar example. Conversely, [satellites]({{ 'satellites/' | relative_url }}) move at orbital velocities approaching several kilometres per second yet often appear to drift smoothly because they are extremely distant.

This relationship means that every estimate of:

* velocity,

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* acceleration,
* object size,
* turning radius, or
* energy requirements

</div>

depends directly on knowing the object's range first. If the assumed distance is wrong by a factor of ten, many derived quantities become wrong by similar or even larger factors. This is one reason that apparent "impossible acceleration" claims based solely on a single optical recording require careful scrutiny before being interpreted as evidence of unusual flight behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geodetic.com/basics-of-photogrammetry/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geodetic.com">[Geodetic Systems+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geodetic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geodetic SystemsBasics of PhotogrammetryThe fundamental principle used by photogrammetry is triangulation. ## How triangulation turns outliers into testable tracks

Adding a second synchronised observing station changes the problem fundamentally.

Instead of one line of sight extending indefinitely into space, two calibrated cameras provide two independent viewing rays. Their geometry constrains where the object can actually be located. Rather than inferring distance from appearance, analysts estimate it directly from intersecting sightlines while accounting for measurement uncertainty.

Once successive positions are reconstructed in three dimensions, the system can calculate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* altitude;
* ground track;
* true velocity;
* climb or descent rate;
* acceleration; and
* uncertainty for every measurement.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://isprs.org/proceedings/XXXV/congress/comm5/papers/188.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isprs.org">[isprs.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isprs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">delay systems can achieve relatively low measurement uncertainty fact...</span></span></span>

</div>

At this point an apparent anomaly becomes a quantitative hypothesis that can be compared with [known aircraft]({{ 'known-aircraft/' | relative_url }}) performance, balloon drift, satellite ephemerides, meteor trajectories or atmospheric effects. Many detections that initially looked exceptional in two dimensions become compatible with ordinary aerial traffic once their real geometry is established. Conversely, if a track remains unusual after distance has been independently measured, it becomes substantially more worthy of further investigation because its kinematics no longer depend on guesswork.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv+2conference.sdo.esoc.esa.int]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Scientific Investigation of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) Using Multimodal Ground-Based ObservatoriesMay 29, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2023</span></span></span>

## Distance measurement introduces its own quality checks

Triangulation is not simply "using two cameras". Reliable reconstruction requires the observing network itself to pass several consistency tests.

If the cameras are accurately calibrated and time-synchronised, reconstructed positions from multiple frames should form a physically plausible trajectory. Large inconsistencies often indicate more ordinary problems such as:

* mismatched targets between stations;
* [timing errors]({{ 'timing-errors/' | relative_url }});
* inaccurate camera [pointing]({{ 'pointing/' | relative_url }});
* lens calibration errors;
* insufficient baseline between observing sites.

Modern photogrammetry and computer vision therefore treat triangulation together with uncertainty estimation rather than assuming every pair of viewing rays intersects perfectly. Real systems estimate the most probable position while propagating calibration and measurement errors through the solution. This makes the resulting three-dimensional track scientifically testable instead of visually persuasive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.04618" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv+2mrcal.secretsauce.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Optimal Triangulation Method is Not Really Optimal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Optimal Triangulation Method is Not Really OptimalJuly 9, 2021 — by SM Nasiri · 2021 · Cited by 11 — Abstract Triangulation refers t...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 2021</span></span></span>

## Why automated UFO detectors should treat 2D outliers as the beginning, not the end

An automated observatory benefits from flagging unusual image tracks early because unusual observations deserve additional measurements. However, the classification pipeline should separate "visually unexpected" from "physically unexplained".

A robust workflow therefore follows a progression:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Detect statistical outliers in image-space behaviour.
2. Attempt multi-station association of the same target.
3. Estimate range through triangulation.
4. Reconstruct the three-dimensional trajectory.
5. Compare measured kinematics against known classes of objects.

</div>

This sequence reduces [false positives]({{ 'false-positives/' | relative_url }}) while preserving genuinely unusual cases for closer examination. Rather than dismissing odd-looking tracks or accepting them as extraordinary, the system asks the more scientific question: what happens to this outlier once its distance is known?

Within automated instrumented UAP detection, that distinction is essential. A strange-looking path on a screen is evidence that merits measurement—not evidence, by itself, that the object in the sky behaved in an extraordinary way.
